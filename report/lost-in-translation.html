Research

Language is part of the product.

Ethereum is global, but many of its products are still built primarily for English speakers only.

We tracked eight months of wallet clicks on ethereum.org, counted the languages that major apps, exchanges and wallets support, and read the translation files several of the most used apps ship.

The pattern is simple. Language can affect what people choose. But translating an interface does not guarantee people understand it.

01 · The opportunity

Sometimes, speaking the user’s language wins the user.

We compared wallet clicks across 18 language groups on ethereum.org.

In 4 markets, language clearly changed the choice. 90% of Japanese readers’ wallet clicks went to wallets that speak Japanese, against 75% for English-language readers. Traditional Chinese, Indonesian and Korean readers showed the same pull, and 4 more languages a smaller one.

In those markets, language is part of the product decision, not just an accessibility feature.

Translation is not universally a growth lever. But in the right market, it can shape which product gets chosen.

03 · The quality problem

Being translated is not the same as being understood.

Crypto products are full of words that look ordinary in English but carry a specific meaning on screen.

Stake. Collateral. Gas. Bridge. Withdraw. Slippage.

Translate the dictionary meaning instead of the product meaning, and an interface can stay grammatically correct while becoming confusing, misleading or simply wrong.

General translation systems are good at language. They are not necessarily good at Ethereum.

A translator needs to know whether “stake” means a wooden post, a financial position or ETH committed to network security. Without that context, even polished translations can fail on the most important words on the screen.

The same app’s Spanish file gets these terms right. Quality can vary by language inside one product, and nothing on the screen gives it away.

Fluency can hide semantic errors.

04 · The duplication problem

Every team keeps translating the same Ethereum words.

Wallets need a word for account. Exchanges need withdraw. Lending apps need collateral. Staking products need validator. Nearly every product needs network, transaction, wallet, address, gas and token.

Yet teams make these decisions on their own. Some translate the term, some keep the English word, some bend it into the grammar of the other language. Each new product starts again.

The same Ethereum concept ends up with different names in different products. For someone learning Ethereum in another language, that can make different products feel like entirely different systems.

Localization is product work.

Language can influence which product people choose. Poor terminology can undermine understanding. Coverage remains uneven. And teams keep solving the same language problems in isolation.

Better localization means deciding where language matters, what Ethereum concepts actually mean, and how to express them consistently.

Ethereum is global infrastructure.
Its products should feel global too.

Research notes

Methodology & sources

Wallet choice on ethereum.org

Data. “Visit website” clicks on ethereum.org/wallets/find-wallet and its language editions, January 1 to September 2, 2026, from ethereum.org’s Matomo analytics. One click per wallet per visit.

Language groups. A reader’s language is their browser language. Traditional Chinese covers zh-TW, zh-HK and zh-MO; Portuguese covers every pt variant. Seven ethereum.org editions (Hindi, Bengali, Urdu, Telugu, Swahili, Tamil and Marathi) had too few clicks and were left out.

Wallet language support. The languages each wallet reports to ethereum.org (wallet-data.ts), as it stood each month. ethereum.org does not verify these.

The comparison. On the English page the wallet list is shuffled for every visitor, so list position cannot explain a difference. For each language, we compared the share of clicks going to wallets that support it from readers with that browser language against readers with an English browser. Ranges are day-level bootstrap 90% intervals. The bars show the relative difference between the two shares. A language is charted when the interval sits above zero and the shares differ by 4 percentage points or more. Japanese and Traditional Chinese rest on about 298 and 207 clicks, so treat them as strong signals rather than precise sizes.

Why not the language pages. On a language edition, wallets that support the language are moved to the top of the list, so their higher share mixes language with position. Portuguese is the clearest case: the Portuguese-supporting wallets get 52% of clicks on the English page and 81% on the Portuguese page, yet Portuguese-browser readers on the English page pick them slightly less often than English readers do (−5 points). Most of that jump is position. The same browser-language comparison on the language pages points the same way but has too few English-browser readers to chart.

Limits. People who read the English page may be more comfortable in English than those who use their own edition, so these numbers probably understate the preference. A preference can also reflect regional brand loyalty rather than language. Clicks show which wallet was chosen, not what happened after. A new catalog layout went live on September 3, 2026 and is not included.

Translation quality and terminology

Data. Shipped translation catalogs in Portuguese, Spanish and Russian, read in June 2026: Uniswap (public repository), 1inch (live locale files), Polymarket (build payload), Aave (public repository), Tornado Cash (live app) and Fluidkey (bundled messages). OpenSea could not be read.

Aave example. From aave/interface src/locales/ru/messages.po. In Aave's public repository; Russian was not offered in the live interface at the July 2026 coverage check. Aave's Spanish translates the same terms correctly (Collateral → Garantía, Repay → Pagar). Each term was checked by translating the shipped word back to English in context.

Divergence. One English source string mapped to more than one distinct translation, within one app or across apps. Exact-key matching can miss reworded strings, so a missing comparison is not proof of a missing translation.

Language coverage

Apps. The ten most used consumer apps on Ethereum and its layer-2 networks by 7-day active addresses (growthepie API, fetched July 10, 2026), excluding centralized-exchange products, wallets, token contracts, infrastructure without a consumer interface, and gaming entries with bot-suspect address counts. Interface languages verified in July 2026 from locale files in public repositories or production JS bundles. English-only verdicts for closed-source apps (Relay, Matcha, Across, Aerodrome) come from inspecting their live bundles and markup; high confidence, short of certainty.

Exchanges. Eleven large centralized exchanges, chosen as widely known large venues rather than from a ranking. Counts are the languages each website declares to search engines, read October 5, 2026 from Europe; Binance from its language menu. Coinbase and Gate block automated reads, so their counts come from their iPhone app listings. Exchanges can offer different languages by region.

Wallets. Ten of the most used wallets by reported user count. Counts are the languages each wallet reports to ethereum.org; OKX Wallet is not listed there and Exodus’s entry says English only, so both use their iPhone app listings.

Counting. English counts as one language. Simplified and Traditional Chinese count as two. Counting languages says nothing about quality.
